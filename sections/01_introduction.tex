\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly embedded in systems that extend far beyond a single prompt--response interaction. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), persistent memory, external tools, multimodal inputs, multi-turn interaction, planning, and multi-agent coordination are now common elements of LLM-based applications. At the same time, deployment introduces constraints that are not visible in conventional benchmark scores, including latency, throughput, cost, resource consumption, privacy, safety, availability, and observability. Evaluating such systems therefore requires more than asking whether a final answer is correct.

The central difficulty is that an observed failure at the output may originate at different points of the system lifecycle. A poor response may result from an ambiguous specification, irrelevant or incomplete retrieved context, an incorrect intermediate decision, an unsuitable tool invocation, a failure to preserve state across turns, or a limitation of the underlying model. Conversely, a system may reach a correct answer through an unreliable or unsafe trajectory. Even when the system behavior is appropriate, the evaluation mechanism may itself introduce error through inconsistent human judgments, unstable automated metrics, biased learned evaluators, or unreliable LLM-based judges. Quality assessment is therefore not only a problem of measuring outputs; it is also a problem of identifying \emph{where} quality is assessed, \emph{how} it is measured, and \emph{whether the measurement itself can be trusted}.

The literature already contains a large and rapidly expanding collection of benchmarks, metrics, evaluator models, test harnesses, agent benchmarks, RAG evaluation methods, and operational evaluation tools. This breadth is valuable, but it also creates fragmentation. Different communities often measure different artifacts with different terminology and assumptions. Prompt robustness may be studied independently of response factuality; retrieval quality may be reported separately from generation quality; agent benchmarks may emphasize final task success while ignoring intermediate trajectories; and production-oriented work may focus on latency or cost without relating these properties to semantic quality. As a consequence, the existence of many evaluation methods does not necessarily imply the existence of an integrated account of system quality.

A second source of ambiguity concerns the role of the evaluator. LLM-as-a-Judge has become a prominent mechanism for evaluating responses, code, reasoning traces, or agent behavior. However, an LLM acting as a judge is an \emph{evaluation method}, whereas the reliability of that judge is a distinct \emph{evaluation target}. A study may use an LLM judge without measuring its consistency, bias, calibration, agreement with humans, or sensitivity to prompting. Treating these situations as equivalent can hide uncertainty in the measurement instrument itself.

A similar distinction is required for the term \emph{end-to-end}. Some studies evaluate a multi-stage system only through its final outcome, providing a useful black-box measure of task success but little information about which stage produced the observed behavior. Other studies explicitly connect quality across stages, for example by relating retrieval quality to answer quality or trajectory quality to task completion. A smaller class attempts to combine several lifecycle stages with operational constraints or evaluator reliability. These forms of evaluation should not be collapsed into a single end-to-end category because they support different kinds of diagnosis and evidence.

This survey addresses these problems through a lifecycle-oriented synthesis of LLM-system quality assessment. Rather than centering the review on a single benchmark family, evaluator technology, or application domain, we separate three complementary concepts: the \emph{object of evaluation}, the \emph{quality dimension being assessed}, and the \emph{evaluation method used to produce the judgment}. We then examine how these elements are combined across the lifecycle of LLM-based systems.

The contributions of this survey are fourfold. First, we organize quality assessment into six lifecycle dimensions: input and specification; context and retrieval; output; conversation and interaction; agent, process, and trajectory; and operational/system quality. Second, we organize evaluation methods independently from these dimensions, allowing the same method---for example, human evaluation or LLM-as-a-Judge---to be analyzed across different targets. Third, we treat evaluator reliability as a meta-evaluation problem and distinguish the use of an evaluator from evidence concerning the quality of that evaluator. Fourth, we characterize the degree of integration in existing evaluation approaches by distinguishing isolated, cross-stage, black-box end-to-end, and lifecycle-integrated assessment.

The resulting perspective is intended to answer a practical question underlying the survey: when an LLM-based system is said to be good, what exactly has been measured, by which mechanism, at which stage, and with what evidence that the resulting judgment is reliable? This question motivates the problem formulation and research questions developed in the following sections.
