\section{Evidence Synthesis by Quality Dimension}
\label{sec:dimensions}

This section will contain the evidence-driven synthesis of the academic corpus after the peer-review audit and final classification freeze. Each subsection will follow the same structure: definition and scope, dominant quality constructs, evaluation methods, representative studies, recurring datasets or benchmarks, evidence of cross-stage relations, limitations, and open gaps.

\subsection{Input and Specification Quality}
\label{sec:input-quality}

\paragraph{Scope and construct.}
Input and specification quality concerns the conditions under which an intended task is communicated to an LLM-based system. Relevant properties include the clarity and completeness of instructions, consistency under meaning-preserving reformulations, sensitivity to prompt templates and examples, and the effectiveness of prompt optimization. Importantly, the object of assessment is not always an intrinsically good or bad prompt. In many studies, the measured construct is instead the \emph{relationship} between input design and subsequent model behavior. A well-formed specification can still yield an incorrect response, just as an underspecified request can occasionally produce a plausible one. Observing a poor output therefore does not by itself establish whether the failure arose from the input or the model.

A useful operational separation is between \emph{sensitivity analysis}, which measures how behavior changes under controlled prompt variations, and \emph{prompt optimization}, which searches for input formulations that improve a target outcome. Both often rely on downstream task performance, but they support different claims. Sensitivity studies test stability or measurement validity; optimization studies seek a better-performing input under a specified objective. Neither automatically measures the general clarity, completeness, or user-level adequacy of a task specification.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to meaning-preserving variation.}
Sclar et al.\ demonstrate that apparently inconsequential formatting choices can substantially change few-shot LLM performance \cite{sclar2024formatting}. Their ICLR study reports differences as large as 76 accuracy points for a tested LLaMA-2-13B setting and introduces \emph{FormatSpread} to characterize performance across sampled plausible formats. The methodological lesson is not that every model exhibits the same magnitude of sensitivity, but that a benchmark result obtained with a single arbitrary template can confound model capability with prompt design. Because format preferences transfer only weakly across models in their experiments, using one common prompt is not necessarily a neutral comparison protocol.

Chatterjee et al.\ make prompt stability more explicit as a measurable property through \emph{POSIX}, an index based on relative changes in response log-likelihood under intent-preserving prompt substitutions \cite{chatterjee2024posix}. Their experiments examine wording, spelling, templates, and few-shot exemplars. They report that larger model size or instruction tuning does not necessarily remove sensitivity, while the most disruptive type of change depends on the task: template alterations are especially influential for multiple-choice questions, whereas paraphrases can be more consequential for open-ended generation. POSIX illustrates a different evaluation strategy from task-accuracy spread: it measures changes in the model's support for a response, rather than simply comparing final task scores.

Liu and Chu extend this line of inquiry with an analytical account of prompt sensitivity \cite{liu2026promptsensitivity}. Using a first-order Taylor approximation of next-token log probabilities, they derive a bound that relates changes in hidden-state representations to changes in output probabilities. Their experiments connect the bound to an existing sensitivity measure and find that prompt templates can affect logits more strongly than the question content in the studied settings. The contribution is primarily \emph{explanatory}: it links input perturbations to internal representations and token-level behavior, rather than proposing a general-purpose measure of specification clarity. Together, these studies suggest that prompt stability is a system-dependent property requiring controlled variation and explicit reporting of the associated output measure.

\paragraph{Optimization and the meaning of a better prompt.}
Prompt optimization takes a complementary perspective. Pryzant et al.\ propose ProTeGi, which uses natural-language critiques of errors on minibatches as textual gradients, edits prompts accordingly, and uses beam search and bandit selection to explore candidates \cite{pryzant2023protegi}. Yang et al.\ introduce Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO), in which an LLM generates candidate instructions from a history of candidate solutions and their evaluated scores \cite{yang2024opro}. Both approaches demonstrate that performance is responsive to prompt design, but their objectives are task-specific: an optimized prompt is better \emph{with respect to a chosen metric, model, and evaluation set}, not necessarily clearer or more reliable for every task or user.

This qualification is not merely theoretical. Zhang et al.\ revisit OPRO with smaller models and report limited effectiveness relative to the setting in which the method was initially demonstrated \cite{zhang2024revisitingopro}. The result cautions against treating prompt-optimization gains as model-agnostic. It also exposes a practical tension: iterative optimization can consume evaluations and model calls, so the benefit of a better final prompt may need to be balanced against search cost. Ramnath et al.\ provide a peer-reviewed synthesis of automatic prompt optimization techniques and organize the design space with a five-part unifying framework \cite{ramnath2025aposurvey}. That survey is important secondary evidence for optimization methods, but it should not be counted as a separate empirical validation of every method it reviews.

\paragraph{From prompt-level assessment to system-level quality.}
The studies above show that input-related properties are commonly evaluated through \emph{downstream signals}: accuracy differences across prompt variants, relative token log-likelihood, or improvements in task scores after optimization. This is valuable cross-stage evidence because it explicitly links input choices to model behavior. It is not, however, equivalent to a complete evaluation of an LLM-based application. For example, Khattab et al.\ introduce DSPy to compile and optimize language-model pipelines against an objective, including settings with multistep reasoning and retrieval \cite{khattab2024dspy}. Their contribution moves beyond an isolated prompt string, yet pipeline-level improvement should not automatically be interpreted as attribution of quality to every internal stage.

\paragraph{Synthesis and implications for integration.}
Three distinctions follow from this initial evidence. First, \emph{robustness to meaning-preserving prompts} is different from \emph{maximizing performance for one selected prompt}; high peak accuracy may coexist with high sensitivity. Second, performance-based prompt evaluation typically mixes properties of the input and of the model, so reporting the prompt variants, underlying evaluator, and task metric is necessary for interpretable comparisons. Third, demonstrating that prompt changes affect task outcomes provides evidence of an input-to-output relationship, but does not by itself establish lifecycle-integrated quality assessment involving retrieval, process traces, evaluator reliability, or operational constraints.

For the present survey, we will therefore code input quality only when input properties are deliberately measured, perturbed, optimized, or explicitly related to downstream quality. We will record the downstream metric separately from the input property, and distinguish observed cross-stage relationships from black-box final-task scoring. Whether this literature systematically underrepresents clarity, completeness, user intent, or deployment-aware prompt assessment remains an \emph{open empirical question} until the complete peer-reviewed corpus is audited.

\subsection{Context and Retrieval Quality}
\label{sec:context-quality}

\paragraph{Scope and attribution.}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) introduces a particularly useful test case for the distinction between component quality and system quality. A retriever must locate information relevant to a query; a generator must use that information appropriately; and the final answer must satisfy the user's task. These requirements overlap but are not interchangeable. A relevant passage may be insufficient to answer the question, an answer may be faithful to a passage that is factually wrong, and a correct answer may arise from model knowledge even when retrieval fails. Consequently, answer correctness, evidence-grounded faithfulness, context relevance, and context coverage are different constructs.

For this survey, \emph{context and retrieval quality} is coded only when the retrieved evidence, its selection, coverage, ranking, or use is explicitly assessed. Merely including a retriever in the architecture is insufficient. Similarly, a final answer score for a RAG pipeline does not automatically establish retrieval-stage quality. The critical question for cross-stage assessment is whether a study independently characterizes retrieval and then analyzes how its properties relate to answer quality, rather than reporting several scores without examining their relationship.

\paragraph{Separating retrieval from generation.}
Es et al.\ introduce RAGAs, a reference-free evaluation framework that distinguishes the relevance of retrieved context from the faithfulness and quality of generation \cite{es2024ragas}. Its contribution is to operationalize different evaluation dimensions without requiring ground-truth reference answers for every example. However, a framework that computes several component-level metrics does not, by that fact alone, demonstrate causal attribution between components; the evaluator choices and validity of each score remain important.

Saad-Falcon et al.\ propose ARES, which separately evaluates \emph{context relevance}, \emph{answer faithfulness}, and \emph{answer relevance} using lightweight learned judges trained on synthetically constructed examples \cite{saadfalcon2024ares}. ARES additionally uses a small human-annotated set with prediction-powered inference to correct aggregate estimates, and reports experiments across eight knowledge-intensive tasks and domain shifts. Its important methodological distinction is that automated judging is paired with explicit human calibration; human evidence is used to estimate evaluation quality rather than assuming a learned judge is intrinsically reliable. The resulting three dimensions should still be interpreted as distinct assessments, not as proof that retrieval alone caused a particular final answer.

Ru et al.\ take a more diagnostic approach with RAGChecker \cite{ru2024ragchecker}. Rather than relying exclusively on a single holistic score, its fine-grained metrics target retrieval and generation behavior and support the analysis of module-specific failures. The authors evaluate eight RAG systems and report meta-evaluation against human judgments. This moves toward stage-aware diagnosis, yet its conclusions remain conditional on the annotated constructs, available reference evidence, and the reliability of the diagnostic metrics themselves. In particular, localizing a failure to a retrieval or generation component is stronger evidence than reporting final-answer correctness, but it is not automatically a causal intervention demonstrating that changing one component will improve the system.

\paragraph{Evaluator choice is part of the evidence.}
A retrieval-aware score is only as interpretable as the measurement procedure that produces it. Cho et al.\ provide a domain-specific empirical comparison in materials science, evaluating graphene-synthesis questions with human experts, BERTScore, LLM-as-a-Judge, and RAGAS \cite{cho2026graphene}. Across 20 questions, the evaluators did not respond equivalently to retrieval augmentation: the study reports that RAGAS detected relative improvements associated with RAG in its setting, while BERTScore and the tested LLM-judge protocol were less discriminative for that purpose. The authors also caution against interpreting RAGAS scores as absolute judgments of scientific answer quality. These results support a claim about \emph{evaluator behavior in a bounded scientific domain}, not universal superiority of one framework.

Neloy and Islam broaden the concern through a controlled meta-analysis that applies 20 RAG evaluation frameworks to the same 200 question--context--answer examples \cite{neloy2026metarag}. They report heterogeneous judgments and a low median pairwise correlation ($r=0.21$) between frameworks. Importantly, this discrepancy should not be simplified into the claim that all evaluators are erroneous: different frameworks may be operationalizing different constructs, applying different scales, or making different assumptions about evidence. It does establish that scores from nominally similar RAG evaluators are not necessarily interchangeable, making construct definition, calibration, and evaluator selection essential parts of quality assessment.

\paragraph{What integrated RAG evaluation currently means.}
Recent secondary studies have already synthesized retrieval and generation within unified RAG-specific taxonomies. Knollmeyer et al.\ review 12 studies and organize context relevance, faithfulness, answer relevance, correctness, and citation quality together with evaluator families and dataset prerequisites \cite{knollmeyer2026rag}. Their framework is a \emph{proposed conceptual synthesis}, not an experimental validation that all its dimensions can be measured reliably in combination. Wu et al.\ provide a wider system-oriented RAG survey that explicitly distinguishes retrieval quality, generation quality, end-to-end robustness, and system-level efficiency; its discussion includes operational measures such as tail latency, throughput, and cost per query \cite{wu2026ragsurvey}. These contributions are particularly relevant prior work for the present survey and prevent any claim that RAG research has ignored multidimensional or system-level assessment.

A further conceptual example is the reliability-aware architecture proposed by García-López and Guillén-Yparrea \cite{garcialopez2026reliabilityrag}. It brings contextual accuracy, faithfulness, refusal, latency, and human review into one proposed quality model for higher-education decision support. Its inclusion illustrates the need to record whether evaluation integration was \emph{proposed}, \emph{discussed}, or \emph{empirically used}: an integrated design can be a valuable research contribution without being treated as validated measurement evidence.

\paragraph{Synthesis for the research questions.}
The RAG literature already contains both modular evaluation frameworks and studies that examine evaluator reliability; the missing piece cannot be asserted to be the mere existence of retrieval--generation links. Instead, this survey asks three more discriminating questions. First, which studies \emph{measure relationships} between retrieval and downstream outcomes, as opposed to presenting side-by-side metrics or a black-box answer score? Second, when a metric or judge is used to substantiate such relationships, what evidence establishes its validity and stability? Third, when trade-offs involving latency, cost, refusal, or operational reliability are introduced, are they jointly evaluated and attributed to system stages, or merely discussed as additional desiderata?

These distinctions provide concrete evidence categories for RQ1--RQ5. They also guard against prematurely labeling any RAG study as \emph{lifecycle-integrated} based only on its architecture or its use of the term end-to-end. The distribution of studies across integration levels, and any claim that particular relationships remain underexplored, will be reported only after the academic corpus and human coding are finalized.

\subsection{Output and Response Quality}
\label{sec:output-quality}

\paragraph{The quality construct.}
Output quality is the assessment of a response or artifact produced by an LLM-based system, but the term cannot be reduced to a single notion of correctness. A response may be fluent yet false, semantically similar to a reference yet omit essential conditions, supported by a retrieved document yet fail to answer the user's actual question, or preferred by human evaluators despite violating a verifiable instruction. We therefore separate \emph{task correctness}, \emph{factual truthfulness}, \emph{faithfulness to provided evidence}, \emph{semantic similarity}, \emph{instruction compliance}, and \emph{human-perceived usefulness}. Safety and fairness may further qualify any of these constructs as cross-cutting properties. The purpose of this distinction is not to multiply labels unnecessarily, but to prevent scores obtained from different measurement assumptions from being interpreted as equivalent evidence of quality.

\paragraph{Reference-based similarity and factual support.}
Traditional lexical matching rewards overlap between an answer and one or more references, whereas contextual-embedding metrics aim to tolerate paraphrase and lexical variation. BERTScore, for example, aligns candidate and reference tokens in a contextual embedding space rather than requiring exact word matches \cite{zhang2020bertscore}. Its ICLR evaluation reports improved correlation with human judgments in the translation and captioning settings investigated. This establishes the usefulness of semantic reference similarity, not factual truth: a response can closely resemble a reference while preserving the wrong entity, quantity, or causal relation.

Adlakha et al.\ make a more directly LLM-oriented distinction by evaluating \emph{correctness} against user information needs and \emph{faithfulness} against provided knowledge in question answering \cite{adlakha2024correctness}. Through human-annotated analysis, they show why conventional exact match and token-level F1 can be inadequate for verbose instruction-following responses. They propose simple overlap-based indicators for the two dimensions and examine abstention when supplied knowledge is irrelevant. Their evidence is particularly important for our taxonomy: output correctness, evidence faithfulness, and appropriate refusal can produce different assessments of the same answer. Nonetheless, because the evidence is explicitly conditioned on provided passages, the measured faithfulness should not be equated with factual verification against an independently established world state.

\paragraph{Atomic factuality and hallucination detection.}
TruthfulQA demonstrates that factuality cannot be inferred from a model's general ability to generate plausible language. Lin et al.\ construct 817 questions spanning 38 categories designed to expose answers that reproduce common misconceptions \cite{lin2022truthfulqa}. This is a targeted benchmark for truthfulness, not a general-purpose measurement of completeness, style, or usefulness.

For long-form generations, Min et al.\ introduce FActScore, which decomposes responses into atomic factual claims and assesses the fraction supported by a trusted knowledge source \cite{min2023factscore}. Atomic decomposition is preferable to a single binary passage label when correct and unsupported assertions coexist. The method also illustrates a key measurement dependency: evidence collection and claim verification form part of the \emph{evaluation pipeline}. FActScore's use of retrieval to check generated statements does not necessarily mean that the generator under evaluation employed retrieval. The source of verification evidence must therefore be distinguished from the context available to the generation system.

Zha et al.\ instead study factual consistency through AlignScore, a learned text-alignment function trained on heterogeneous supervision and evaluated on 22 datasets \cite{zha2023alignscore}. It estimates support or consistency between pieces of text, making it suitable for comparing an output with supplied evidence across tasks. The broader limitation remains construct-level: \emph{consistency with a source} is not synonymous with the source's \emph{independent factual correctness}. Manakul et al.\ propose SelfCheckGPT, which uses disagreement among stochastic samples from a black-box generator as an indicator of potentially non-factual statements \cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt}. This reduces dependence on external reference documents, but mutual agreement among outputs is not, by itself, proof of truth; shared or repeated errors may remain stable across samples. Thus, these three approaches provide distinct signals---atomic evidence support, learned alignment, and self-consistency---rather than alternative names for one universal hallucination score.

\paragraph{Human preferences and model-based judges.}
Open-ended tasks such as summarization and dialogue also require criteria that are not always exhausted by reference matching or factual verification. G-Eval uses a strong LLM with a structured form-filling protocol to score natural-language generations and reports improved correspondence with human judgments in its evaluated summarization setting \cite{liu2023geval}. Its analysis simultaneously raises the possibility of preference bias toward LLM-generated text. This is an instructive instance in which the \emph{output} is the principal quality target, the \emph{LLM judge} is the method, and the reported agreement and bias analysis are evidence about the \emph{evaluator} itself. These aspects must be coded separately.

Zheng et al.\ further examine judge-based assessment of conversational assistants with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, including human preference comparisons and known judge biases \cite{zheng2023mtbench}. Their contribution extends beyond individual response metrics, but it does not establish that an evaluator aligned with aggregate human preference will also verify factual statements, adhere to every instruction, or identify failures within a multi-stage application. As in the previous section, evaluator agreement is meaningful only relative to the target construct and the protocol used to elicit judgments.

\paragraph{Verifiable constraints and executable outcomes.}
Some output requirements are amenable to deterministic checks. Gu et al.\ introduce DINGO, an instruction-following evaluation benchmark organized around a fine-grained hierarchy of real-world user instructions \cite{gu2024dingo}. It reveals that treating instruction compliance as a single aggregate capability can obscure which kinds of constraints a model fails to satisfy. The boundary with input quality is important: a paper is classified as evaluating \emph{input/specification quality} only when properties of the specification are themselves measured or manipulated, not merely because a fixed instruction is supplied to test the produced response.

The same distinction applies to generated code and software artifacts. SWE-bench evaluates whether a generated code change resolves a real-world repository issue under executable tests \cite{jimenez2024swebench}. Task-resolution success provides a stronger observable outcome than stylistic similarity to a reference patch, but does not reveal whether an agent used safe tools, efficient steps, or a reliable trajectory unless those are measured separately. In other words, final executable correctness can be a valid \emph{black-box task outcome} even when process and operational quality remain unobserved.

\paragraph{Beyond a single output score.}
A useful counterpoint to narrowly defined output metrics is HELM, which evaluates language models across scenarios using multiple desiderata including accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency \cite{liang2023helm}. This provides important evidence that multi-metric evaluation existed before the recent surge of agentic and RAG-specific frameworks. However, multi-metric coverage should not automatically be counted as \emph{lifecycle-integrated assessment}: reporting accuracy alongside efficiency, for example, need not connect input specifications, retrieved evidence, intermediate behavior, or evaluator reliability through an explicit attribution model.

\paragraph{Implications for quality integration.}
The evidence suggests that several seemingly similar output scores address genuinely different questions: \emph{Is the answer right? Is it supported by the supplied evidence? Does it satisfy the request? Is its style useful to a person? Does executable behavior meet the task objective?} No single metric should be assumed to answer all of them. Moreover, an output benchmark cannot explain failures in retrieval, specification, tool use, or operation unless those relations are explicitly investigated. The present survey will therefore record the measured output construct and its evaluation method independently, distinguish output evaluation from judge meta-evaluation, and treat cross-stage and lifecycle-integrated claims as empirical questions for final corpus coding rather than conclusions established by the existence of a multi-metric scorecard.

\subsection{Conversation and Interaction Quality}
\label{sec:conversation-quality}

\paragraph{From response quality to interaction quality.}
A conversation is not simply a set of independent outputs. User goals may change, earlier constraints may remain binding, new information may correct previous assumptions, and the system may need to recover from errors or ask for clarification. Consequently, a model that answers each turn plausibly in isolation may still provide a poor overall interaction. We distinguish \emph{cross-turn consistency}, \emph{recollection and state maintenance}, \emph{adaptation to feedback or revised instructions}, \emph{long-term memory}, and \emph{interaction-level goal completion}. These constructs partly overlap with input, context, and output quality but are not reducible to any of them. In coding this dimension, we therefore require evaluation of an actual turn dependency, persistent state, or interactive feedback loop; merely presenting several unrelated prompts does not suffice.

\paragraph{Cross-turn dependencies and error propagation.}
Kwan et al.\ introduce MT-Eval to examine four recurring interaction patterns: recollection, expansion, refinement, and follow-up \cite{kwan2024mteval}. Importantly, they construct single-turn counterparts for 1,170 multi-turn queries and compare ten models. The observed performance degradation in multi-turn settings makes the evaluation more informative than simply aggregating answer scores across turns. Their analysis identifies both the distance to relevant earlier information and the propagation of prior errors as factors affecting conversational performance. The paired evaluation design provides evidence of a \emph{cross-turn effect}, although it should not be interpreted as causal attribution to a single internal subsystem.

Deshpande et al.\ approach the same problem from challenging realistic interactions. MultiChallenge targets four situations requiring instruction following, context allocation, and in-context reasoning across turns \cite{deshpande2025multichallenge}. Its authors report that the evaluated frontier systems remained below 50\% accuracy, despite high performance on existing conversation benchmarks; the top system in their experiments achieved 41.4\%. The result should be understood within the specific challenge construction and evaluated model versions, not as a universal accuracy estimate for deployed assistants. MultiChallenge also employs instance-level rubrics and LLM-based judges, with comparisons to experienced human raters. This creates two separately coded objects: \emph{conversation quality} as the target and \emph{judge agreement} as meta-evaluation evidence. Importantly, the existence of a judge does not itself establish its reliability.

CMT-Eval extends benchmark design to Chinese-language, persona-sensitive conversational settings \cite{tian2025cmteval}. Its speech-act-driven dataset contains 596 dialogues and 4,431 turns, with variation in user personas and interaction challenges. The work illustrates that evaluation scenarios and conversational conventions are themselves methodological choices: a test suite restricted to one language, interaction style, or persona distribution cannot be presumed to cover all forms of user adaptation. The relevant concern is not only final-turn correctness but whether the assistant responds appropriately to evolving dialogue intentions.

\paragraph{Memory across sessions.}
Memory-oriented evaluations introduce a further distinction between retaining conversational information and using it correctly when needed. Maharana et al.\ introduce LoCoMo, a dataset of very long-term conversations grounded in personas and temporal event graphs \cite{maharana2024locomo}. Its dialogues average approximately 600 turns and 16,000 tokens across as many as 32 sessions, and the benchmark probes question answering, event summarization, and multimodal dialogue generation. Their experimental comparisons indicate substantial difficulties with long-range temporal and causal understanding despite improvements from long-context models or retrieval-augmented strategies. A single correct answer to a memory question does not reveal whether the relevant event was retrieved correctly, whether chronology was reconstructed, or whether a generated answer relied on unsupported assumptions.

LongMemEval offers a more explicitly decomposable perspective \cite{wu2025longmemeval}. Its 500 questions test information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention. Beyond reporting a drop in memory accuracy over sustained interactions, Wu et al.\ analyze memory systems through \emph{indexing}, \emph{retrieval}, and \emph{reading}, and investigate design interventions at these stages. This is an important cross-stage counterexample to any claim that conversational evaluation is limited to final response scoring. However, such studies primarily analyze long-term conversational memory: they do not automatically integrate evaluator reliability, agent trajectories, and operational quality into a common system-wide measurement.

\paragraph{Feedback, simulation, and interactive task success.}
Static transcript evaluation differs from an environment in which the user or system reacts to previous behavior. MINT explicitly evaluates multi-turn interaction with tools and natural-language feedback \cite{wang2024mint}. Its controlled setup allows LLMs to execute Python-based tools and receive feedback simulated by GPT-4, with tests spanning reasoning, coding, and decision-making. Its experiments show that stronger single-turn capability need not predict greater benefit from feedback or tools. In our taxonomy, interaction quality and process/trajectory quality can both be evaluated in a study when the evidence genuinely targets each; tool availability by itself is not enough to infer that tool-use quality was measured.

For goal-oriented conversations, Yao et al.\ present $\tau$-bench, which simulates user--agent exchanges in retail and airline domains with tools and policy constraints \cite{yao2025taubench}. Rather than asking a judge whether the final message sounds helpful, the benchmark compares the resulting database state with the annotated goal state and uses the $\mathrm{pass}^{k}$ metric to characterize reliability over repeated trials. The paper reports substantial inconsistency under repeated executions. This is stronger verification of interactive task completion than a single natural-language score, but it remains a \emph{task-level outcome}: a correct final state does not necessarily establish that every intermediate action was safe, efficient, or policy-compliant unless the relevant trace is also examined. The setup additionally depends on the validity of simulated user behavior and the specified database goals.

Zhou et al.\ study another form of interaction with SOTOPIA, an environment for role-play scenarios involving communication, cooperation, competition, and social goals \cite{zhou2024sotopia}. SOTOPIA-Eval assesses social intelligence in simulated human/agent interactions, thus targeting qualities that may be difficult to express through exact-match outcomes. The contribution broadens the interpretation of interaction quality beyond information recall or task completion, but the ecological validity of a simulated social environment and the reliability of judgment protocols remain separate questions. Together, MINT, $\tau$-bench, and SOTOPIA demonstrate that interaction evaluation can involve dynamic feedback, environment state, human-like social goals, and evaluators with very different measurement assumptions.

\paragraph{Synthesis for lifecycle integration.}
These studies demonstrate why conversation quality is a distinct lifecycle target rather than an enlarged output benchmark. Interaction failures may arise from forgotten instructions, outdated or incorrectly updated memory, inappropriate responses to user feedback, or incorrect actions within an evolving environment. Evidence of \emph{cross-turn deterioration} is not equivalent to evidence of \emph{memory-stage attribution}; likewise, task success over several turns is not proof of correct intermediate behavior. The measurement method also matters: reference answers, rubric-based LLM judges, environment-state checks, and repeatability statistics target different constructs and require different validation.

For our final synthesis, we will therefore code conversation/interaction quality alongside, but separately from, context retrieval and agent/process quality. We will record whether earlier state is explicitly manipulated or assessed, whether feedback changes subsequent behavior, whether tasks are judged turn-by-turn or from final outcomes, and whether simulated users and evaluators have independent reliability evidence. The existence and prevalence of \emph{lifecycle-integrated} conversation-quality frameworks remain empirical questions for the fully screened corpus; they cannot be inferred from a long dialogue, a multi-metric scoreboard, or the use of an LLM simulator alone.
