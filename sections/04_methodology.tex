\section{Review Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Review Design}

The review is designed as a \emph{systematic mapping study} (SMS) with evidence-based narrative synthesis, following methodological guidance for mapping studies in software engineering \cite{petersen2015mapping}. The aim is to classify a broad research area, identify the distribution of evaluation targets and methods, and examine cross-stage relations rather than calculate a pooled intervention effect. We use the PRISMA 2020 reporting framework to account transparently for identification, screening, eligibility, and included studies \cite{page2021prisma}. The PRISMA-S extension guides the documentation of information sources, executable search strings, search dates and limits, supplementary discovery, and deduplication \cite{rethlefsen2021prismas}. PRISMA and PRISMA-S are \emph{reporting guidelines}; neither substitutes for conducting a rigorous and independently auditable mapping.

The procedure separates corpus construction, publication-status verification, scope screening, evidence extraction, and validation. Automated methods support triage and extraction at scale, but automated classifications are not treated as ground truth. The final report will include the per-stage counts and exclusion reasons only after the historical blocks, temporal updates, bibliographic deduplication, and human adjudication have been reconciled.

The review is organized into four evidence tracks. The \emph{historical academic corpus} contains studies retrieved during the initial search campaign. A \emph{temporal update} captures publications appearing after the historical cutoff. A \emph{secondary-study track} records surveys and reviews, including whether their own evidence bases mix peer-reviewed and non-peer-reviewed material. Finally, a separate \emph{practice and tooling track} captures evaluation platforms, frameworks, and industry evidence that are relevant to current practice but are not counted as independent peer-reviewed academic evidence.

\subsection{Historical Corpus and Temporal Update}

The historical retrieval phase produced 2,662 records distributed across 12 search blocks. File-level deduplication identified 2,561 unique PDFs by SHA-1, corresponding to 60,858 pages in the retrieved archive. These figures describe the historical \emph{retrieved corpus}; they must not be interpreted as the final number of studies included in the academic synthesis because publication status and final eligibility are audited separately.

The operational cutoff for the historical corpus is May 25, 2026. The temporal update therefore begins on May 26, 2026 and is rerun before manuscript freeze. To reduce the risk of missing papers affected by indexing delays, the update procedure may include a short overlap with the end of the historical period; duplicates are then removed during metadata and file-level reconciliation.

Searches are organized around query families rather than a single umbrella string. These families target general LLM evaluation, input and prompt quality, RAG and contextual quality, output evaluation, evaluator reliability, multi-judge approaches, agent evaluation, operational and serving quality, integrated or end-to-end assessment, interoperability and agent protocols, human/expert evaluation, and surveys or reviews.

\subsection{Peer-Review and Publication-Status Gate}

Peer review is an explicit eligibility criterion for the primary academic corpus. Each candidate study receives a publication-status label distinguishing peer-reviewed journal, conference, or workshop publications from preprint-only manuscripts, theses, technical reports, book chapters, non-archival workshop material, and other grey literature. When the publication status cannot be established reliably, the record is routed to manual verification rather than automatically included or excluded.

The existence of an arXiv or other preprint version is not itself grounds for exclusion. If a peer-reviewed version of record exists, the study remains eligible and the peer-reviewed version is treated as canonical. Conversely, a preprint does not inherit peer-reviewed status because it is cited or discussed by a peer-reviewed survey.

For secondary studies, we additionally record the nature of their underlying evidence base using a secondary-evidence policy. This distinguishes reviews restricted to peer-reviewed literature from reviews that mix peer-reviewed studies with preprints or broader grey literature. The review itself may remain eligible, while the status of the evidence it synthesizes remains visible.

\subsection{Scope Screening}

Scope screening assigns each record to one of three relevance classes. \textbf{Core} studies directly evaluate one or more dimensions of quality of an LLM or LLM-based system, or explicitly investigate relations among those dimensions. \textbf{Adjacent} studies use an LLM primarily as a tool or component for evaluating an external-domain object, or present a transferable evaluation method without making LLM-system quality the principal object of investigation. \textbf{Exclude} is used when the work neither evaluates LLM-system quality nor provides a directly relevant evaluation contribution.

This distinction is intentionally independent of application domain. A study in medicine, energy, IoT, education, or software engineering can be Core when it directly evaluates the LLM-based subsystem. Conversely, the presence of an LLM in an application does not make the study Core when the principal outcome concerns an external object and the quality contribution of the LLM is not separately assessed.

\subsection{Coding Scheme}

For each eligible study, the extraction schema records: (i) study type; (ii) primary evaluation object; (iii) quality dimensions; (iv) evaluation methods; (v) evaluation protocols; (vi) experimental regime; (vii) integration level; and (viii) supporting evidence.

Study type distinguishes empirical experiments, surveys or reviews, systematic reviews, benchmarks, framework or method papers, tools or platforms, datasets or resources, conceptual or position papers, case studies, mixed studies, and other designs.

The primary evaluation object is coded separately from the quality dimension. The object identifies whether the study primarily evaluates an LLM-based system, an evaluator or judge, or an external-domain object. Quality dimensions are then coded independently across input/specification, context/retrieval, output, conversation/interaction, agent/process/trajectory, and operational/system quality. Evaluator reliability is represented separately as a meta-evaluation target.

A dimension is marked as evaluated only when its quality is explicitly measured, compared, manipulated, or analyzed. Architectural presence alone is insufficient. Therefore, the use of a prompt does not imply evaluation of input quality; the presence of RAG does not imply evaluation of retrieval quality; deployment does not imply evaluation of operational quality; and the use of an LLM judge does not imply evaluation of judge reliability.

Evaluation methods are recorded independently from their targets. Method families include deterministic or rule-based evaluation, lexical/reference metrics, semantic/reference metrics, learned evaluators, human evaluation, LLM-as-a-Judge, multi-judge or ensemble approaches, task- or environment-based evaluation, adversarial or red-team evaluation, perturbation or mutation testing, trace-aware evaluation, hybrid methods, and statistical reliability/meta-evaluation.

Evaluation protocol is coded separately when identifiable. Protocol categories include absolute scoring, pairwise comparison, ranking, rubric-based scoring, binary classification, critique, preference judgment, and task-success verification. Experimental regime captures distinctions such as offline versus online evaluation, development versus production, component versus end-to-end analysis, black-box versus trace-aware evaluation, single-turn versus multi-turn interaction, reference-based versus reference-free evaluation, and continuous monitoring where applicable.

\subsection{Integration Coding}

Integration is coded independently from multidimensionality. A study that reports several quality metrics is not automatically considered integrated. The integration taxonomy contains four levels:

\begin{description}
\item[None:] The study evaluates one component or dimension without explicitly connecting its quality to another lifecycle stage.
\item[Cross-stage:] At least two lifecycle stages or quality dimensions are explicitly related, compared, or causally analyzed.
\item[Black-box end-to-end:] A multi-stage workflow is evaluated from input to final outcome without decomposing or attributing the contribution of internal stages.
\item[Lifecycle-integrated:] At least three lifecycle dimensions or stages are jointly evaluated, including evaluator reliability or operational/system quality, with explicit cross-stage attribution, relation, or joint aggregation.
\end{description}

For integrated claims, we additionally record whether the integration is \emph{used}, \emph{proposed}, or \emph{discussed}. This prevents conceptual frameworks and survey taxonomies from being counted as empirically validated integrated assessments.

\subsection{Evidence Extraction and Full-Text Review}

Title and abstract are used for initial scope and coding decisions when they contain sufficient evidence. Records are escalated to full-text review whenever the abstract does not support a confident classification, when integration claims depend on implementation details, when the role of an evaluator is ambiguous, or when exact metrics and protocols are required.

Full-text extraction is page-aware. Supporting passages are stored with page references and, where possible, exact quotations for later verification. Metrics, datasets, evaluated models, judge models, system components, and explicit cross-stage relationships are recorded separately. This evidence-first representation allows uncertain classifications to be revisited without repeating the complete screening process.

\subsection{Human Calibration and Validation}

The coding scheme is calibrated using three partially overlapping human-review batteries. Each battery contains 40 studies. Twenty studies are shared across the three batteries to support direct inter-reviewer agreement analysis, while twenty studies in each battery are unique in order to increase coverage without requiring every reviewer to classify the same large sample. Thus, each reviewer evaluates 40 studies while the three batteries collectively cover 80 distinct records.

Reviewers initially classify title and abstract without access to automated labels. They may explicitly mark a case as uncertain or indicate that the abstract is insufficient; such cases are routed to full-text adjudication instead of forcing a categorical decision. The calibration focuses on relevance, primary evaluation object, individual quality dimensions, evaluation methods, whether the evaluator itself is under study, cross-stage relations, end-to-end behavior, integration level, and confidence.

Agreement is analyzed separately for exclusive categorical variables and multi-label fields. With three independent human reviewers, nominal agreement is summarized using Krippendorff's alpha where appropriate. Multi-label dimensions are evaluated as individual binary decisions rather than collapsed into a single composite category. Human--human agreement is treated as the primary reliability evidence for the coding protocol; automated classifiers are compared with the human consensus separately and are not counted as human raters.

\subsection{Academic Evidence and Practice Evidence}

Peer-reviewed literature and practice-oriented evidence are synthesized separately. Evaluation platforms, vendor documentation, practitioner benchmarks, and independent tool comparisons can provide important evidence about feature coverage, operational behavior, and current workflows, but they are not treated as equivalent to peer-reviewed validation of evaluator correctness. Where tools are discussed, the review therefore distinguishes capability claims, vendor validation, independent practitioner comparisons, independent quantitative benchmarks, and peer-reviewed validation.

This separation is particularly important for evaluation frameworks whose availability and adoption may substantially exceed the amount of published evidence supporting their measurement validity.
