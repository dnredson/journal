\section{Relationship to Existing Surveys}
\label{sec:prior-surveys}

The secondary literature on LLM evaluation is already substantial, and a broad novelty claim such as \emph{no comprehensive survey of LLM evaluation exists} would be indefensible. A verification search in Google Scholar revealed two especially important general evaluation surveys that must be considered explicitly in addition to the RAG-, agent-, and judge-centered reviews already identified in our corpus. Chang et al. provide an early comprehensive account organized around \emph{what}, \emph{where}, and \emph{how} to evaluate LLMs \cite{chang2024evaluation}. More recently, Ibrahimo\u{g}lu et al. conduct a PRISMA-guided systematic survey of evaluation methods, benchmarks, frameworks, and deployed evaluation practice \cite{ibrahimoglu2026systematic}. Saleh et al. review LLM efficiency, applications, hardware, and performance metrics \cite{saleh2025evaluating}, while G\'{o}mez et al. focus specifically on benchmark capability coverage, scientific benchmark quality, and saturation \cite{gomez2026benchmarks}. These works are substantially closer to our scope than domain-specific surveys and therefore form an essential novelty baseline.

The present survey differs from these general reviews in the unit of analysis. Existing general surveys predominantly organize evaluation around model capabilities, tasks, datasets, benchmarks, evaluator types, or evaluation frameworks. Our organizing object is instead the \emph{LLM-based system lifecycle}. We ask whether quality is assessed at input/specification, context/retrieval, output, interaction, agent/process, or operational stages; whether these stages are explicitly related; and whether the measurement mechanism itself has been validated. Thus, the distinction between \emph{what is evaluated}, \emph{how it is evaluated}, and \emph{whether the evaluator can be trusted} is combined with a separate analysis of \emph{integration across stages}.

Specialized surveys remain important because they provide deeper coverage of individual parts of this lifecycle. Yehudai et al. survey LLM-agent evaluation across capabilities, benchmarks, and developer frameworks \cite{yehudai2026surveyagents}. Nageshwaran et al. provide a validity-centered systematic survey of agent benchmarks, including scoring paradigms, environment topology, non-determinism, contamination, and execution cost \cite{nageshwaran2026agent}. Knollmeyer et al. synthesize RAG evaluation dimensions, metrics, evaluator families, datasets, and retrieval--generation relationships \cite{knollmeyer2026rag}. Nad\u{a}\c{s} surveys LLM-as-a-Judge techniques, prompting protocols, multi-judge pipelines, bias, calibration, and robustness \cite{nadas2026judges}. These surveys overlap strongly with individual dimensions or method families in our taxonomy, but none uses the full lifecycle and cross-stage quality-attribution problem as its primary organizing question.

To make this distinction testable rather than rhetorical, Table~\ref{tab:survey-rq-coverage} compares the closest prior surveys against the six research questions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}. A full mark indicates that the corresponding question is a substantive organizing objective of the survey; a partial mark indicates that the topic is discussed or analyzed within a narrower scope but is not addressed as a general LLM-system question; and a dash indicates that the question is outside the survey's substantive scope. The classification is deliberately conservative: a paper is not credited with an RQ merely because it mentions a related concept.

The table should not be read as a ranking of survey quality. For example, Nageshwaran et al. provide substantially deeper validity analysis for agent evaluation than a lifecycle-wide survey can reasonably provide, and Knollmeyer et al. provide finer-grained coverage of RAG evaluation. The comparison instead identifies the difference in \emph{question coverage}. RQ1 and RQ2 are already addressed strongly by general LLM-evaluation surveys, so they are not, by themselves, sufficient novelty claims for the present work. The differentiating contribution lies primarily in the joint treatment of RQ3--RQ5: explicit cross-stage integration, measurement-instrument reliability, and operational quality within the same lifecycle model. RQ6 then synthesizes these three views into an evidence-based gap analysis.

This distinction also changes how we formulate the paper's novelty. We do not claim to be the first survey to catalog LLM evaluation methods, benchmarks, capabilities, RAG metrics, agent benchmarks, or LLM judges. Instead, we ask whether these mature but specialized evaluation literatures can be reconciled into a common system-quality model, and how much empirical evidence actually exists for relationships among lifecycle stages. In particular, we distinguish \emph{multidimensional evaluation} from \emph{integrated evaluation}: reporting prompt robustness, answer correctness, latency, and judge scores in the same study does not establish how those quantities interact. Our integration taxonomy---isolated, cross-stage, black-box end-to-end, and lifecycle-integrated---is designed to expose that distinction.

The Google Scholar verification also identified several additional reviews that are relevant but less direct novelty competitors, including broad LLM surveys with evaluation sections, benchmark-quality reviews, safety-centered taxonomies, inference-serving surveys, and domain-oriented reviews. These will be retained as supporting related work or dimension-specific evidence, while the comparison table prioritizes surveys that most directly overlap with the six research questions.
